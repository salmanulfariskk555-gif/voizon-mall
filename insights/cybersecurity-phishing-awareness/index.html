Phishing Awareness for Small Businesses: Spot Suspicious Emails and Messages

Train staff to recognise phishing, fake invoices, account takeover attempts, malicious links and safe reporting procedures.

VOIZON Digital Solutions · Updated 2026-11-17

Phishing messages try to persuade a person to reveal credentials, approve a payment, open a malicious file or share confidential information. They can arrive by email, SMS, messaging apps, social media or phone. Technical filters help, but staff also need a simple way to pause and verify unusual requests.

Recognise common warning signs

Look for unexpected urgency, unusual payment instructions, mismatched sender addresses, unexpected attachments, links that do not match the claimed destination and requests for passwords or one-time codes. Spelling mistakes can be a clue, but polished messages can also be fraudulent.

Verify payment and bank-detail changes

Treat new bank details, urgent transfers and changes to supplier payment instructions as high-risk. Verify through a known phone number or trusted channel already on file, not the contact details supplied in the suspicious message.

Check links and attachments safely

Do not open unexpected attachments or enter credentials after following an unsolicited link. Navigate to important services using a known bookmark or manually typed address. Report suspicious messages to the organisation's designated contact.

Protect accounts

Use multi-factor authentication, unique passwords and a password manager where appropriate. Never share one-time codes. Limit administrator privileges and remove access that is no longer required.

Create a simple reporting process

Tell staff exactly where to forward or report suspicious messages. Encourage quick reporting without blame. Preserve the message and relevant details so IT support can assess whether other accounts or devices may be affected.

Respond quickly to a suspected compromise

If credentials were entered, change them from a trusted device, revoke active sessions where possible and notify the relevant service provider or IT team. If money was transferred, contact the bank immediately using official contact details. Preserve evidence and follow the incident plan.

Practise realistic scenarios

Run short awareness sessions using safe examples relevant to the business, such as fake invoices, delivery notifications or shared-document invitations. Focus on the verification behaviour employees should use, not just memorising warning signs.

Key takeaway

The most useful phishing defence is a repeatable habit: pause, verify through a trusted channel, protect credentials and report suspicious messages quickly. Make reporting easy and practise the process before an incident.
